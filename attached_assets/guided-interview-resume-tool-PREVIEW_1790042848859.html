Most "guided" tools are forms with a friendly face.

They ask for your title, dates and duties, which your resume already has. So what you send says what your job was, not what you did in it.

What most tools ask
  • Job title?
  • Key responsibilities?
  • Any achievements?
What an interview asks
  • What was on fire when you joined?
  • What would have broken without you?
  • Who noticed?
"Any achievements?" is the question that makes people close the tab.

One answer, three follow-ups

The bullet you need is usually three questions down.

  1. Surface

    You start with

    "I managed the warehouse team."

  2. Context

    What changed while you were there?

    "We hired a lot for peak season."

  3. Ownership

    Who trained the new people?

    "Me, I guess. Twelve of them. I made a checklist so I'd stop repeating myself."

  4. Impact

    Is that checklist still used?

    "Yeah. It's the standard onboarding now."

The bullet

Trained 12 peak-season hires and wrote the onboarding checklist that became the site's standard process.

She didn't mention the twelve hires because she didn't think they counted. They counted.

Five things people check before they start talking

Fair enough. You're about to tell a piece of software about your job.

"I'm bad at talking about myself."

Good, you won't have to. You answer questions about what happened, the way you'd tell a friend. The interview does the translating into resume language.

"Do I have to use my voice?"

No, typing works too. Talking is usually faster, because people ramble more out loud, and the rambling is where the details hide.

"I don't have time to do my whole career."

You don't do it all at once. Pick one role, stop when you need to, and come back later. It picks up where you left off.

"Is this a resume builder with a chat window?"

A builder asks you to fill in fields. The interview keeps asking until it has the problem, your part, the scale and the result. Then it writes.
